# **Beowulf - Less by Just as Much** - A Note on Lines 1282b–1287a

The passage appears in the Nowell Codex (British Library Cotton MS Vitellius A. xv), copied around the year 1000. It sits at a narrative crux: Grendel's amother has just retaliated for her son's death by seizing Æschere, Hrothgar's trusted advisor. The poet interrupts the action to compare the terror she inspires to the terror inspired by a weaponed man.

The Old English text is as follows:

> Wæs se gryre læssa  
> efne swa micle, swa bið mægþa cræft,  
> wiggryre wifes, be wæpnedmen,  
> þonne heoru bunden, hamere geþuren,  
> sweord swate fah, swin ofer helme  
> ecgum dyhttig, andweard scireð.

A word-by-word breakdown gives the literal components:

| Old English | Direct Gloss |
| :--- | :--- |
| *Wæs se gryre læssa* | Was the terror less |
| *efne swa micle* | exactly by so much |
| *swa bið mægþa cræft* | as is the strength of maidens |
| *wiggryre wifes* | the war-terror of a woman |
| *be wæpnedmen* | compared to a weaponed-man |
| *þonne heoru bunden* | when a bound blade |
| *hamere geþuren* | forged by hammer |
| *sweord swate fah* | sword stained with blood |
| *swin ofer helme* | the boar-crest over the helmet |
| *ecgum dyhttig* | with edges mighty |
| *andweard scireð* | opposing shears/cuts through |

The literal syntactic translation runs:

> *"The terror was less, by exactly as much as is the strength of maidens, the war-terror of a woman, compared to a weaponed-man, when a bound blade, forged by the hammer, a sword stained with blood, strong of edge, cleaves the opposing boar-crest above the helmet."*

The grammar forces a direct proportionality. The correlative pair *efne swa micle... swa* requires the degree of "less" to match exactly the degree of the strength differential. Let \( T_w \) be the war-terror of the woman, \( T_m \) the war-terror of the weaponed-man, \( S_w \) the strength of the woman, and \( S_m \) the strength of the man (implied as the baseline). The syntax states:

> \( \frac{T_w}{T_m} = \frac{S_w}{S_m} \)

Or equivalently, if \( r = S_w / S_m \), then \( T_w = r \times T_m \). The terror scales down proportionally with the physical capacity. The poet offers a quantitative comparison, not a moral one.

That is the surface grammar. A glossed translation can make the cultural subtext more visible without altering the core equation:

> [Insomuch as she was a monster and a grieving mother, Grendel's mother restrained her predatory instincts to operate within the limits of human law, trading indiscriminate slaughter for a surgical, eye-for-an-eye retaliation; she acted alone out of tragic necessity because she had no male kin left to execute the vengeance owed to her lineage, thus validating the human system of blood-feud even as she broke its boundaries] the terror was less, by just as much as is the strength of maidens, the war-terror of a woman [who by rigid social law was expected to absorb grief passively as a diplomatic peace-weaver, not to act as an aggressive blood-feud avenger] compared to a weaponed-man, when a bound blade [the ring-hilted weapon of an elite warrior sworn by blood-oaths to his lord and the collective duty of war including being an avenger], forged by the hammer, a sword stained with blood, strong of edge, cleaves the opposing boar-crest above the helmet, [which shattered ward signifies the violent destruction of his sacred protection]. [Thus her terror was proportionally diminished, scaled exactly to the gap in raw physical might between her sex and a male warrior's.]

The bracketed additions draw on contexts that the poem supplies elsewhere: the blood-feud system, the peace-weaver role assigned to noble women, the material culture of ring-hilted swords, and the protective function of the boar-crest (attested archaeologically at Sutton Hoo). None of these are explicit in the clause itself; they are the cultural grammar surrounding it.

Several interpretive readings have been built on this passage. One takes the proportion at face value, treating it as a straightforward reflection of early medieval assumptions about physical capacity. Another reads it as dramatic irony: the poet asserts a diminished terror only to subvert it in the underwater fight. A third observes the lexical asymmetry in how *aglæca* is translated—"monster" for her, "hero" for Beowulf—and takes the numerical "less" as a pointed exposure of masculine bias. Each reading has its internal logic. The grammar does not adjudicate between them.
